# Chapter 10 — Limits, Criticisms, and When Not to Use Balanced OKRs

A framework designed to resist metric gaming can itself become a game. Balanced OKRs must therefore be judged by whether it improves organizational decisions relative to its cost, not by whether its terminology is adopted.

## More measurement can make things worse

Every guardrail has a cost: data collection, definition disputes, reviews, and attention. A team with dozens of protected metrics may become unable to act. Prefer a few high-consequence guardrails and keep exploratory diagnostics out of the scoring process.

## Guardrails can conceal value judgments

What counts as “unacceptable” harm is not purely technical. Different stakeholders may disagree about short-term profit, customer experience, risk, and employee workload. Naming a threshold does not settle the ethical or strategic question. Decision rights and escalation procedures must be explicit.

## False confidence in weak data

An apparently precise metric may have biased selection, missing records, inconsistent definitions, or a small denominator. Treat confidence and data provenance as part of the review. Do not convert a noisy fluctuation into a punitive decision.

## A guardrail can be gamed too

Once people know the boundary, they may optimize just above it or redefine the population. Independent audits, segmentation, and periodic revalidation can help, but none makes the problem disappear. Balance requires judgment, not merely more thresholds.

## The danger of veto culture

A hard boundary can become an excuse to block all change. Distinguish non-negotiable obligations from thresholds that trigger investigation and authorized risk decisions. Google's SRE error-budget approach illustrates a disciplined way to discuss reliability risk while still enabling delivery [R6].

## Not all work belongs in an OKR

Routine operations, statutory obligations, incident response, professional ethics, and basic service commitments may be managed better through operating standards, service-level objectives, checklists, or direct accountability. Forcing every duty into a quarterly aspirational goal obscures the distinction between *must do* and *want to improve*.

## Evidence is still emerging for this proposal

Balanced OKRs, as defined in this book, is a proposed synthesis of ideas from goal-setting, measurement theory, experimentation, software delivery, and systems thinking. The referenced sources support important component ideas, but **they do not establish that this exact three-part framework has been experimentally validated**. Organizations adopting it should treat their rollout as an experiment and compare the decision quality and administrative burden against simpler alternatives.

## Final test

At the end of a quarter, ask:

1. Did we produce a meaningful outcome?
2. What did we damage, displace, or defer?
3. How confident are we that our actions caused the result?
4. What did we learn about the validity of our measures?
5. What decision will change next?

If these questions produce clearer, more responsible decisions, Balanced OKRs has earned its place. If the process produces only additional reporting, simplify or stop it.
