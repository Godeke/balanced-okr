# Chapter 3 — Mechanisms, Incentives, and Gaming

A goal system changes what people have reason to do. People notice what gets praised, funded, compared, or punished. Before approving a goal, ask what a reasonable person would do under those rules.

## Three distinct distortions

**Target substitution** occurs when the metric displaces the underlying purpose. Call counts can rise while useful conversations do not. **Local optimization** occurs when a team improves its score by imposing costs on others. **Metric decay** occurs when a once-useful proxy stops tracking the outcome as practices and markets change.

Goodhart's work on monetary control is a historical starting point for thinking about unstable relationships under policy pressure [R2]. Campbell's analysis of indicators in social decision-making offers a related warning about distortion when indicators carry high stakes [R3]. Neither should be reduced to a universal claim that all targets are useless.

## What the sales meeting rewards

What Matters advises against tying OKR attainment directly to bonuses [R11]. Paying people according to how well they meet a target they helped set gives them a reason to negotiate an easy target. Removing the OKR bonus still leaves several reasons to chase the number.

Consider the hypothetical insurance campaign. The company keeps OKR scores out of pay decisions. Its existing commission still pays on new business, and the commercial director opens each sales meeting by praising the person who wrote the most policies. When renewals come in below the Guardrail, it is the top producer who first points out the decline. Everyone in that meeting gets to see what telling the truth costs.

The director credits the producer for raising the issue, records that the issuance KR was achieved, and explains why expansion is paused. In a separate compensation discussion, the director asks whether the commission plan keeps rewarding sales the company has reason to reconsider. In regular coaching, the producer and manager can examine actual customer conversations and how the producer handled the emerging evidence. A ranking by OKR score would tell them little about either.

Schweitzer, Ordóñez, and Douma found in a laboratory experiment that participants who fell just short of an assigned goal were particularly likely to misrepresent their results, including when the goal carried no financial reward [R12]. That finding gives a manager a reason to examine the pressure created by a public ranking. It cannot tell the manager why a particular producer's customers left.

## Design against predictable strategies

Before adopting a metric, imagine an intelligent, well-intentioned participant trying to maximize it with the least effort. Could a team redefine “qualified,” defer difficult cases, split work items artificially, shift incidents into a different category, or select an easy denominator? Could it improve this quarter by moving costs into the next?

Keep the original eligibility rules and denominators available for review. Ask someone who uses the measure to explain when it stopped describing the work accurately. They may spot a change in practice long before it appears in an aggregate report.

## Don't reward permanent green

A portfolio with no missed ambitious goals may indicate weak ambition or selective reporting. Celebrating every miss can also excuse poor execution. Ask separately how much the outcome changed, whether the original goal was worth pursuing, and how well the team exercised judgment. A well-run experiment that overturns a plausible assumption can settle an expensive decision.

Locke and Latham's goal-setting research describes how specificity and challenge can support performance under suitable conditions [R4].

## Periodic metric revalidation

Give each recurring measure an owner, a reason for using it, and a review date. Suppose calls per representative rose after the sales meeting introduced a leaderboard. At the next review, the team finds that representatives now make several brief attempts per account and that the count no longer predicts useful conversations. Retire the leaderboard. Keep a record of outreach attempts only where it helps answer an operational question, such as whether customers in an outreach trial actually received a call.

**Practice exercise.** Ask participants to invent three ways to hit a proposed Key Result while delivering little value. Record the most plausible one. What would you change in the measure, the Guardrail, or the way leaders recognize people?
