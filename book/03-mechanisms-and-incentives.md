# Chapter 3 — Mechanisms, Incentives, and Gaming

A goal system does not merely observe behavior; it changes the payoffs attached to behavior. People notice what gets praised, funded, compared, or punished. The challenge is therefore a mechanism-design problem: what conduct will reasonable participants adopt under the rules we create?

## Three distinct distortions

**Target substitution** occurs when the metric displaces the underlying purpose. Call counts can rise while useful conversations do not. **Local optimization** occurs when a team improves its score by imposing costs on others. **Metric decay** occurs when a once-useful proxy stops tracking the outcome as practices and markets change.

Goodhart's work on monetary control is a historical starting point for thinking about unstable relationships under policy pressure [R2]. Campbell's analysis of indicators in social decision-making offers a related warning about distortion when indicators carry high stakes [R3]. Neither should be reduced to a universal claim that all targets are useless.

## Removing bonuses is not enough

Tying a person's bonus formula directly to an ambitious self-set target invites sandbagging. Keeping OKRs separate from compensation is a sensible design choice. Yet employees also respond to promotions, reputation, scarce resources, and management attention. A nominally non-compensatory score can become a shadow ranking.

Leaders should separate three evaluations: **outcome attainment**, **quality of goal design**, and **quality of judgment and contribution**. A difficult experiment that honestly rejects a false hypothesis can demonstrate excellent judgment. A trivial green target may demonstrate very little.

## Design against predictable strategies

Before adopting a metric, imagine an intelligent, well-intentioned participant trying to maximize it with the least effort. Could a team redefine “qualified,” defer difficult cases, split work items artificially, shift incidents into a different category, or select an easy denominator? Could it improve this quarter by moving costs into the next?

Countermeasures include stable definitions, cohort comparisons, independent sampling, auditable data, and guardrails. They also include social practices: invite staff to report that a metric has become misleading without treating that observation as disloyalty.

## Don't reward permanent green

A portfolio with no missed ambitious goals may indicate weak ambition or selective reporting. Conversely, indiscriminate celebration of misses can excuse poor execution. A balanced review asks whether targets were appropriately challenging, assumptions were plausible, execution was competent, and evidence was learned.

Locke and Latham's goal-setting research supports the importance of specificity and challenge under suitable conditions [R4]; it does not imply that any numerical goal is automatically beneficial.

## Periodic metric revalidation

Every recurring metric should have an owner, a rationale, and a date for revalidation. Ask whether it still predicts the intended outcome, whether the definition changed, whether participants have adapted, and whether a better measure is now available. Retiring a familiar but misleading measure is a success of governance.

**Practice exercise:** Run a “gaming workshop.” Ask participants to invent three ways to hit a proposed key result without delivering value. Document the most plausible strategy and redesign the metric or guardrail accordingly.
