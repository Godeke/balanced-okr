# Chapter 8 — A Practical Adoption Playbook

An organization should not begin by converting every existing target into an OKR. Start with a few consequential outcomes, establish definitions, and learn whether the process improves decisions.

## Phase 1: a calibration quarter

Select a small number of teams representing different kinds of work: a commercial team, an operational team, and a technology team. Ask each to draft one or two Balanced OKRs. Train reviewers to distinguish outcomes, guardrails, and diagnostics.

Measure the *quality of the measurement system*: Are baselines reliable? Do people understand the denominator? Are guardrails actionable? Have dependencies been accepted? Avoid using early scorecards to rank employees.

## Phase 2: test and refine

During the next quarter, examine actual decision points. Did guardrails reveal a tradeoff? Did diagnostics change an intervention? Were frontline hypotheses tested rather than mandated? Did a shared dependency overwhelm capacity?

Remove measures that no one uses. Improve ambiguous thresholds. Encourage teams to admit when a proposed relationship between activity and outcome lacks evidence.

## Phase 3: portfolio coherence

Once local examples are working, connect them to strategic outcomes. Review the portfolio for duplication, unowned outcomes, conflicting incentives, and demand on shared teams. Maintain a modest library of examples, including *failed* OKR designs and what was learned.

## Phase 4: institutionalize review, not bureaucracy

Publish a regular retrospective on metric validity and side effects. Assign review dates to recurring measures. Establish a mechanism to surface bad incentives without punishment. Use the simplest tooling that preserves definitions, baselines, decisions, and historical outcomes.

## Sample workshop agenda

1. **Define success:** Participants write the outcome in plain language.
2. **Red-team the metric:** Others invent ways to improve the score without delivering value.
3. **Find collateral damage:** Identify customers, teams, or future periods that could bear costs.
4. **Choose evidence:** Separate KRs, guardrails, and diagnostics.
5. **Check capacity:** Confirm cross-team commitments.
6. **Set a review date:** State what would cause the OKR to be revised or retired.

## Adoption risks

A poor rollout can produce metric proliferation, performative compliance, or unproductive debates over scoring. Leaders should demonstrate that the process can lead to **stopping** an initiative, not just reporting on it. If a team never changes course in response to new evidence, the OKR process may be theater.

**Practice exercise:** Pilot the process on one existing strategic initiative. Record how many decisions changed because of balancing information, and whether the cost of collecting that information was justified.
