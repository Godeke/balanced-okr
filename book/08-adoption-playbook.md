# Chapter 8 — A Practical Adoption Playbook

Start with a few consequential outcomes. Establish definitions and see how the process affects an actual decision before extending it across the organization.

## The first quarter

Invite a few teams that do different kinds of work. Ask each to bring one live decision and draft an Objective, a Key Result, and a Guardrail on the [worksheet](worksheet.md). A commercial team deciding whether to expand a campaign has a reason to come back and use what it wrote.

Have someone from another team try to interpret the draft. Can they tell which customers count, when the result will be known, and who can act on it? A confusing denominator or an unassigned decision is worth discovering during the first quarter. Give the authors feedback while they can use it.

## The second quarter

Examine actual decisions. Did a Guardrail reveal a tradeoff? If the reviewer examined a particular product or channel, did that evidence change the response? Did a frontline proposal change what leadership funded? Did a participant have the capacity it promised? Keep a delayed review on its owner's calendar even as the next quarter starts.

Remove measures that no one uses. Improve ambiguous thresholds. Encourage teams to admit when a proposed relationship between activity and outcome lacks evidence.

## Reviewing the portfolio

Once local examples are working, connect them to strategic outcomes. Review the portfolio for duplication, unowned outcomes, conflicting incentives, and demand on shared teams. Maintain a modest library of examples, including *failed* OKR designs and what was learned.

## Keeping the practice useful

Publish a regular retrospective on metric validity and side effects. Assign review dates to recurring measures. Establish a mechanism to surface bad incentives without punishment. Use the simplest tooling that preserves definitions, baselines, decisions, and historical outcomes.

## Sample workshop agenda

1. Have the team write its Objective in plain language. Ask a colleague what would change for a customer or employee.
2. Draft the KR. Invite someone doing the work to show how its number could improve while the customer sees no benefit.
3. Ask who could bear the cost. Write a Guardrail with a boundary or review trigger. Add a breakdown only if the reviewer needs it to assess that Guardrail.
4. Ask each participant for the capacity and timing it can offer. Identify which other work would have to move.
5. Rehearse quarter close with a pending Guardrail. Name the person who may authorize expansion, the reason they would record, and the date the evidence is due.
6. Set a review date for the measure itself. Agree on what evidence might lead the team to revise or retire it.

## Adoption risks

A team can spend its review debating a score everyone knows will change nothing. Ask the person accountable for the decision what they would do if the next cohort crossed the Guardrail. If they cannot name an action, revisit the measure or give the decision to someone with authority to act.

**Practice exercise.** Take one completed worksheet to a portfolio review. Which specific decision did it help someone make, and was that decision worth the work of gathering the evidence?
