# Chapter 4 — Causality, Evidence, and Discovery

Before making an activity mandatory, ask what difference it makes and for whom. A count of calls can tell a manager that outreach happened. The decision to invest another hour in outreach depends on what that hour achieves.

## A chain of assumptions

In *Measure What Matters*, Doerr compares two employees with a call target. The employee who makes fewer calls brings in more customers [R10]. A sales manager can use that observation before setting the next target. Which accounts benefit from a call, and would the same accounts have bound coverage without the extra outreach?

Imagine a manager proposing a call on every quote. A representative who handles small accounts points out that many agents have already supplied everything underwriting needs. Colleagues who handle large, complex accounts use calls to clarify coverage questions. The representatives propose testing the extra call in each group before it becomes a standing requirement. Their observations give the manager a hypothesis to investigate.

## Execution and discovery OKRs

An **execution OKR** is appropriate when there is credible evidence that an intervention works and the challenge is implementation. A **discovery OKR** is appropriate when the main uncertainty is whether, where, or why an intervention works.

For discovery, the desired result can be enough evidence to make a decision. Define in advance what evidence would support extending, narrowing, or ending the pilot.

## A pilot the sales manager can act on

Comparing accounts that received calls with those that did not leaves the original question open. Representatives may choose to call accounts that are already more likely to bind. In this hypothetical pilot, the team defines its small and large account groups before enrollment. Within each group, it randomly assigns eligible accounts to the usual service or to the usual service plus one proactive call. Both groups continue to receive help they request.

For each assigned group, the team measures the share of quoted accounts that bind within 30 days and total staff minutes per assigned account. It retains every assigned account in the comparison, including those that never answered. A record of attempted and completed calls helps interpret how much extra outreach actually took place. The statistician's question is whether assignment to extra outreach changed the outcome. The manager's question is whether the additional binds justify the additional time.

Before the pilot, sales and operations agree how large a gain would justify the staff time in each group, when to evaluate the evidence, and how they will protect service to other customers. At review, a gain large enough to justify the cost can support expanding calls in that segment. If the plausible gains are all too small to cover the time, discontinue the extra call there. If the evidence still spans both useful and negligible gains, report that uncertainty and decide whether another round is worth its cost. None of these decisions requires a company-wide call quota.

The population, comparison, and observation window matter even where random assignment is impractical. In an observational study, document why accounts received different treatment and which differences remain unresolved. The NIST/SEMATECH handbook covers experimental design and the role of comparison groups [R7].

## Leading indicators are hypotheses

A leading indicator earns its place when it reliably anticipates a later outcome. Record why the team expects that relationship, what evidence supports it, and when it will check whether the relationship still holds.

While renewal is pending, complaints and early cancellations can guide a customer review. Follow the original cohort to first renewal to evaluate the renewal Guardrail and learn whether the early signals were informative.

## Use evidence proportionate to stakes

Not every workflow tweak requires a randomized trial. Small reversible experiments can rely on lightweight observation. Large expensive or irreversible policy changes deserve stronger evidence. The standard should rise with cost, risk, and the opportunity for misleading inference.

**Practice exercise.** For a proposed activity-based KR, draw the chain from activity to customer or business outcome. Mark each untested link. Rewrite the Objective as a discovery question if the most important link remains uncertain.
