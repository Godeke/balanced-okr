# Chapter 1 — The Problem with One-Sided Success

> **A team can hit every Key Result and still leave the business worse off.**

## Where Balanced OKRs starts

Objectives and Key Results help a team name a change worth making and agree on evidence of progress. John Doerr already asks teams to consider the cost of optimizing a single measure. In the racing example in *Measure What Matters*, the pit crew tracks both pit-stop time and errors. Faster stops count for little if the car leaves the pit with a wheel fitted incorrectly. [R1], [R9]

Balanced OKRs builds on that pairing. When a consequence matters enough to change a decision, give it an explicit role as a **Guardrail**. Before starting, agree on what would trigger a review, who can change course, and when the evidence will be available. This agreement matters most when an initiative has met its target and someone wants to expand it.

## The hidden cost of new business

Consider a hypothetical sales campaign. On March 31 the commercial director hears that the team has issued 1,150 qualified new annual policies, exactly the quarter's target. A sales manager asks for the budget to expand the campaign. The director knows how many policies sold. Even the earliest policies in the cohort reach their first anniversaries the following January.

What can the director responsibly say about the campaign? Three questions call for different evidence.

| Question | What the team can establish |
| --- | --- |
| Did we meet the Key Result? | The issuance records show whether the team wrote the targeted volume. |
| Was the growth acceptable? | First renewal helps the director judge whether the business the campaign acquired was sustainable. Until the cohort matures, that judgment is provisional. |
| What caused the result? | A decline in renewal warrants a response. Understanding whether pricing, product mix or sales practice contributed takes further investigation. |

The director can recognize the team's achievement today and reserve the expansion decision. In Chapter 2, the team writes down who may authorize expansion while renewal is pending. In Chapter 6, the director has to act on the evidence.

## The hidden cost of velocity

Software teams face the same choice on a shorter clock. Smaller batches and automated releases can get changes into production sooner. Skipping tests can shorten the same interval. Looking at lead time alone, the two changes may look equally promising.

A team that pairs lead time with the share of changes requiring a rollback or an emergency fix can see a reliability cost. If the failure rate crosses the agreed threshold, the team can examine the affected releases and decide whether to adjust the rollout. Enough days saved on delivery cannot erase a material reliability breach.

## Three ways a metric can fail

**People game the measure.** An employee can meet a call quota by choosing agents who will get off the phone quickly. A manager who praises the count makes that choice reasonable even if longer conversations would help customers more.

**Teams optimize locally.** Sales can increase throughput by sending incomplete submissions to underwriting. The sales team's improvement is real. So is the additional work for the underwriters who complete the submissions.

**The measure loses its meaning.** A leading indicator that once predicted success can become unreliable as products, markets, or people's behavior change. A team should periodically check whether the measure still tells it anything about the outcome it cares about. Looking only for misconduct will miss this case.

## How much belongs in an OKR?

Use the smallest set that can guide the decision. The **Objective** gives the team a meaningful change to pursue. A **Key Result** makes a desired improvement observable. A **Guardrail** identifies a material adverse consequence and the boundary or review trigger associated with it. If renewal deteriorates, for example, a breakdown by channel can help the director evaluate the Guardrail. Collect that detail when someone has a use for it.

Thresholds require judgment. A small group of policies may produce volatile renewal rates, and changing market conditions may affect everyone. Verify the data and investigate plausible explanations before assigning responsibility. Legal and safety obligations continue to apply regardless of how the OKR is written.

## How leaders respond

People notice what earns a bonus, a promotion, a bigger budget or a public thank-you. Leaders can keep the OKR score out of pay formulas and still teach the team to hide a troubling number by praising only the person who brought in the most business. Reporting a concern about the new policies is a contribution to the acquisition decision. Managers can recognize that contribution when it happens.
